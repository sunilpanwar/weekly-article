🐕 Siberian Husky – Beautiful but Challenging in India’s Heat

The Siberian Husky is one of the most stunning dog breeds – with thick fur, bright blue or multi‑coloured eyes, and a wolf‑like face. But they were bred for freezing Arctic conditions, not Indian summers. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see many Huskies suffering from heatstroke because owners do not understand their needs. If you live in a hot city, a Husky will need air conditioning, early morning walks, and a lot of exercise. They are not a good choice for first‑time dog owners.

🐾 Key things to remember: Huskies cannot tolerate Indian heat. They need air conditioning during summer. They are escape artists – you need a very secure yard. They also need 2+ hours of exercise daily.

What does a Siberian Husky look like?

  • Size: Medium‑large – 16–27 kg. Height 50–60 cm.
  • Coat: Very thick double coat. Sheds heavily twice a year (blows coat).
  • Colours: Black and white, grey and white, red and white, or pure white.
  • Eyes: Blue, brown, one of each (heterochromia), or parti‑coloured.
  • Build: Athletic, strong, built for pulling sleds.

Why Huskies are difficult in India

  • Heat intolerance: Their thick coat is designed for -40°C. In Indian summer, they overheat very quickly. They must live in air conditioning.
  • High energy: They need 2+ hours of vigorous exercise daily. Without it, they become destructive – they dig, escape, and chew.
  • Escape artists: They can jump over high fences, dig under gates, and open latches. You need a 6‑foot+ secure yard.
  • Independent nature: They are not eager to please like Labradors. Training is difficult and requires patience.
❄️ Dr. Deepa’s warning: “I have treated Huskies with heatstroke after just 10 minutes of walking at 10 AM. If you cannot afford 24/7 air conditioning and cannot exercise them before sunrise and after sunset, please do not get a Husky.”

Common health problems in Huskies

  • Heatstroke: The biggest danger. Signs: heavy panting, dark red tongue, weakness, collapse. Emergency!
  • Eye problems: Cataracts, progressive retinal atrophy (PRA), corneal dystrophy. Regular eye checks needed.
  • Hip dysplasia: Less common than in other large breeds, but still possible. Keep them lean.
  • Zinc deficiency: Huskies have trouble absorbing zinc, causing skin lesions around the face and paws. Supplement only under vet guidance.
  • Bloat (gastric torsion): Feed two small meals daily, no exercise after eating.

Grooming – very high maintenance

  • Daily brushing: Use an undercoat rake. During shedding season (twice a year), brush twice a day – they will “blow” their coat in clumps.
  • Never shave a Husky: Their coat protects them from heat and sun. Shaving ruins their natural cooling system and causes sunburn.
  • Bathe only when needed (2–3 times a year). Too many baths dry out their skin.
  • Clean ears weekly. Trim nails every 3–4 weeks.

Exercise requirements – very high

Huskies were bred to run for miles. Without enough exercise, they become destructive and may run away.

  • Walks: Only before 7 AM and after 7 PM. Use the paw test (Week 13). Keep walks to 15–20 minutes in summer, but do multiple short walks.
  • Running: They love to run – a fenced, secure area is essential. A dog park with high fencing is ideal.
  • Mental stimulation: Puzzle toys, nose work, obedience training – they are smart but stubborn.
  • Indoor exercise: On very hot days, play tug‑of‑war, hide treats, or train tricks indoors.

Training and behaviour

  • Huskies are independent and can be stubborn. They are not good for first‑time owners.
  • Use positive reinforcement – harsh methods make them fearful or aggressive.
  • Socialise them early – they can be friendly with people but may chase small animals (cats, squirrels).
  • Never let them off‑leash in an unsecured area – they will run and ignore your calls.

Feeding guidelines

  • High‑quality dog food – they need good protein for their energy levels.
  • Adult Husky: 2–3 cups of dry food per day (divided into two meals). Adjust based on activity.
  • Do not overfeed – a fat Husky has joint problems and overheats faster.
  • Always fresh, cool water – add ice cubes in summer.

Real story from our clinic

A family in Mumbai bought a Husky puppy online. They did not have air conditioning and walked him at 9 AM. One day, after a 15‑minute walk, the dog collapsed with seizures. His temperature was 107°F. We tried cooling measures, but he died within an hour. The family was devastated. The father said, “We had no idea they couldn’t handle the heat. We loved him, but we failed him.” Please do not let this happen to your Husky.

Should you get a Siberian Husky?

Yes, only if you:

  • Live in a cool climate (hills) or have 24/7 air conditioning.
  • Have a very secure, high‑fenced yard.
  • Can commit to 2+ hours of daily exercise (early morning and late night).
  • Are an experienced dog owner – not your first dog.
  • Can manage heavy shedding and escape attempts.

Otherwise, choose a heat‑tolerant breed like an Indie dog or even a Labrador.

Action for Week 7

  1. If you already have a Husky, check your air conditioning – is it working? Never leave them in a hot room.
  2. Walk only before 7 AM and after 7 PM – no exceptions.
  3. Check your fencing – can your Husky dig under or jump over? Reinforce it.
  4. If you are considering a Husky, visit a shelter first – many are surrendered because owners cannot handle them.
