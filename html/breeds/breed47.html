🐕 Australian Shepherd – Energetic Herding Dog, Prone to Eye Disease and Epilepsy

The Australian Shepherd (often called “Aussie”) is a medium‑sized, intelligent herding dog. Despite the name, they were developed in the United States. They are energetic, loyal, and love to work. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see Aussies that are wonderful for active families. However, they have some serious health problems. They are prone to eye diseases (collie eye anomaly, cataracts) and epilepsy (seizures). They also need a lot of exercise and mental stimulation – a bored Aussie can become destructive or anxious. They are not for first‑time owners or apartments.

🐾 Key things to remember: Australian Shepherds need daily vigorous exercise and mental challenges. They are prone to eye disease – yearly eye exams are essential. Epilepsy is common – learn to recognise seizures. They shed heavily – brush 2–3 times a week.

What does an Australian Shepherd look like?

  • Size: Medium – 20–30 kg. Height 45–55 cm.
  • Coat: Medium‑long, double coat – colours: blue merle, red merle, black, red, with or without white and tan markings.
  • Build: Lean, muscular, deep chest, strong legs, and often a docked or natural bobtail.

Why Australian Shepherds are popular – and challenging

  • Intelligent and trainable: They excel at obedience, agility, and herding.
  • Energetic: They need 1–2 hours of vigorous exercise daily.
  • Loyal and protective: They bond strongly with family and can be wary of strangers.
  • High grooming needs: Their thick coat sheds heavily and needs regular brushing.
👁️ Dr. Deepa’s warning: “Australian Shepherds have a high risk of collie eye anomaly (CEA) – a genetic disease that can cause blindness. Yearly eye exams by a veterinary ophthalmologist are important. Also, epilepsy is common – if your dog has a seizure, time it and bring them to us.”

Common health problems in Australian Shepherds

  • Collie eye anomaly (CEA): Inherited eye defect – can range from mild to blindness. Yearly eye exams recommended.
  • Cataracts: Can cause blindness – surgery may be an option.
  • Epilepsy (seizures): Common. Seizures can be controlled with medication. If a seizure lasts more than 5 minutes, it’s an emergency.
  • Hip and elbow dysplasia: Keep them lean.
  • Deafness: Merle Aussies may be deaf – BAER test recommended.

Grooming – moderate to high

  • Brush 2–3 times a week: Use a slicker brush and undercoat rake. During shedding season (spring, autumn), brush daily.
  • Bathe once a month or when dirty – use a good dog shampoo. Dry thoroughly.
  • Clean ears weekly – their erect ears can trap dirt.
  • Trim nails every 3–4 weeks. Brush teeth weekly.

Exercise and mental stimulation – very important

  • Physical exercise: 1–2 hours daily – running, fetch, frisbee, hiking, or swimming.
  • Mental stimulation: Puzzle toys, nose work, obedience training, agility, herding (if possible).
  • Secure yard: A fenced yard is essential – they can jump and dig.
  • Not for apartments: They need space and an outlet for their energy.

Training and behaviour

  • Australian Shepherds are very trainable but need an experienced owner. Use positive reinforcement (treats, praise).
  • Start socialisation early – they can be shy or reactive if not exposed to many people and situations.
  • They may try to herd children, other pets, or cars – teach a “leave it” command.
  • They can develop separation anxiety – do not leave them alone for long hours.

Feeding guidelines

  • High‑quality dog food – they need good nutrition for their energy levels.
  • For an adult Australian Shepherd, 2–3 cups of dry food per day (divided into two meals). Adjust based on activity.
  • Keep them lean – obesity worsens joint problems and seizure management.
  • Always fresh, clean water.

Real story from our clinic

An Australian Shepherd named “Blue” came in for a seizure that lasted 3 minutes. His owner was terrified. We diagnosed epilepsy and started him on anti‑seizure medication. Blue now has seizures only once every few months, which are short and manageable. His owner says, “I didn’t know Aussies are prone to seizures. Now I know what to do.”

Should you get an Australian Shepherd?

Yes, if you:

  • Are an experienced dog owner – not your first dog.
  • Have time for 1–2 hours of vigorous exercise plus mental games daily.
  • Can provide a job – agility, obedience, herding, or advanced tricks.
  • Have a securely fenced yard.

Maybe not, if you:

  • Live in an apartment without a yard – they need space.
  • Work long hours – they will become destructive and anxious.
  • Are a first‑time owner – they need experienced handling.
  • Want a calm, low‑energy dog – Australian Shepherds are very active.

Action for Week 47

  1. If you have an Australian Shepherd, schedule an eye exam (CEA, cataract screening).
  2. Learn the signs of a seizure – if your dog has one, time it and call us.
  3. Increase mental stimulation – try a new puzzle toy or teach a new trick.
  4. If you are thinking of getting an Australian Shepherd, ask the breeder about eye, hip, and epilepsy screening in the parents.
