Week 10 • Cat Breed

🐱 Maine Coon – Large, Friendly, but Prone to Hip Dysplasia and Heart Disease

The Maine Coon is one of the largest domestic cat breeds. They look like a gentle giant – with a long, shaggy coat, tufted ears, and a big bushy tail. They are known as “gentle giants” because they are friendly, patient, and good with families. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see Maine Coons that are loving and playful, but they also have some serious health problems – especially hip dysplasia and a heart condition called HCM. If you want a Maine Coon, you need space (they are big) and regular vet checkups for their heart and joints.

🐾 Key things to remember: Maine Coons grow slowly – they take 3–4 years to reach full size. They need regular brushing (their long coat mats easily). They are prone to hip dysplasia and heart disease – yearly screening is important.

What does a Maine Coon look like?

  • Size: Very large – males 6–8 kg, females 4–6 kg. Length up to 1 meter (including tail).
  • Coat: Long, thick, and water‑repellent. Shorter on shoulders, longer on belly and tail.
  • Colours: Many colours – brown tabby is most common, also black, white, red, silver.
  • Ears: Large, pointed, with tufts of fur on the tips (like a lynx).
  • Build: Muscular, broad chest, strong legs, and a long, fluffy tail.

Why Maine Coons are popular in India

  • Gentle nature: They are good with children, dogs, and other cats. Very patient.
  • Playful: They stay playful even as adults – they like fetch, chasing toys, and playing with water.
  • Intelligent: They can learn tricks and enjoy puzzle toys.
  • Dog‑like behaviour: They often follow their owners around and greet them at the door.
🩺 Dr. Deepa’s warning: “Maine Coons are prone to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) – a thickening of the heart muscle. Regular heart screenings (ultrasound) are important. Also, hip dysplasia is common in this breed – watch for limping or difficulty jumping.”

Common health problems in Maine Coons

  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM): A heart disease that causes the heart walls to thicken. Can lead to heart failure. Yearly heart ultrasound is recommended.
  • Hip dysplasia: Loose hip joints cause pain and arthritis. Keep them lean and give joint supplements.
  • Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA): A genetic disease that causes muscle weakness. Responsible breeders test for this.
  • Polycystic kidney disease (PKD): Less common than in Persians, but still possible. Kidney screening recommended.
  • Dental disease: Brush teeth weekly.

Grooming – high maintenance

  • Brush 2–3 times a week: Use a steel comb and a slicker brush. Mats form easily behind the ears, under the armpits, and on the belly.
  • During shedding season (spring/autumn): Brush daily – they shed heavily.
  • Bathe once a month or when dirty – use a cat‑safe shampoo.
  • Clean ears weekly – their large, hairy ears can trap dirt.
  • Trim nails every 2–3 weeks. Brush teeth weekly.

Exercise and space needs

Maine Coons are active but not hyper. They need room to move.

  • Space: They are large cats – they need a home with enough floor space and tall cat trees.
  • Play: 15–20 minutes of active play twice a day – feather wands, fetch, puzzle toys.
  • Climbing: They love to climb – tall cat trees (6+ feet) or wall shelves are great.
  • Water play: Many Maine Coons enjoy playing with water – a shallow pan with floating toys is fun.

Feeding guidelines

  • High‑quality cat food – Maine Coons need good nutrition for their large size.
  • For an adult Maine Coon, 1/2 to 3/4 cup of dry food per day (divided into two meals) plus wet food. Adjust based on size and activity.
  • Do not overfeed – obesity worsens hip dysplasia.
  • Consider joint supplements (glucosamine) from middle age – ask us.
  • Always fresh, clean water – use a wide, shallow bowl (their wide whiskers dislike deep bowls).

Real story from our clinic

A 6‑year‑old Maine Coon named “Simba” came in for limping. He was having trouble jumping onto his favourite chair. X‑rays showed hip dysplasia – his hip joints were loose and arthritic. We advised weight management (he was overweight), joint supplements, and a ramp to help him climb. Simba lost 1 kg over 3 months, and his limping improved. His owner also started yearly heart ultrasounds after we explained HCM risk. Simba is now 9 years old and still active. His owner says, “I didn’t know big cats have hip problems. Now I watch his weight carefully.”

Should you get a Maine Coon?

Yes, if you:

  • Have enough space – a large home or apartment with high cat trees.
  • Can commit to regular grooming (brushing 2–3 times a week).
  • Can afford yearly heart and joint screenings.
  • Want a gentle, dog‑like, playful cat.

Maybe not, if you:

  • Live in a very small apartment with no climbing space.
  • Cannot handle long fur and shedding.
  • Are not prepared for potential expensive health issues (heart, hips).
  • Want a low‑maintenance cat (an Indie cat is much easier).

Action for Week 10

  1. If you have a Maine Coon, check their hips – do they have difficulty jumping or climbing stairs?
  2. Feel their chest – is their heartbeat regular? Any coughing or heavy breathing? Schedule a heart check.
  3. Brush their coat today – check for mats behind the ears and under the armpits.
  4. If you are thinking of getting a Maine Coon, research breeders who test for HCM and SMA.
