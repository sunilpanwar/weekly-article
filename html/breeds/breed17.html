🐕 Rottweiler – Strong, Protective, Prone to Hip Dysplasia and Bloat

The Rottweiler is a powerful, confident dog. They are loyal protectors of their family. With the right training, they are calm, loving, and gentle. But without training, they can become aggressive. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see Rottweilers that are wonderful family dogs – but only if the owner takes training seriously. They also have some health problems – especially hip and elbow problems, and a dangerous stomach condition called bloat.

🐾 Key things to remember: Rottweilers need early training and socialisation – they are not for first‑time owners. They are prone to bloat – feed two small meals daily, no exercise after eating. Keep them lean to protect their joints.

What does a Rottweiler look like?

  • Size: Large – males 45–55 kg, females 35–45 kg. Height 55–70 cm.
  • Coat: Short, dense, straight – easy to groom. Sheds moderately.
  • Colours: Black with tan or mahogany markings on face, chest, and legs.
  • Build: Muscular, broad chest, strong head. Looks powerful.

Why Rottweilers are popular in India

  • Loyal and protective: They will guard your home and family with their life.
  • Confident and calm: When well‑trained, they are not hyper or nervous.
  • Good with children: They can be gentle with family children if raised together.
  • Low grooming: Short coat needs only weekly brushing.
🛡️ Dr. Deepa’s warning: “Rottweilers are powerful dogs. Without early training and socialisation, they can become aggressive. They need a confident owner who can be a strong leader. This is not a breed for a timid or first‑time owner.”

Common health problems in Rottweilers

  • Hip and elbow dysplasia: Common in large breeds. Causes pain, limping, arthritis. Keep them lean and give joint supplements.
  • Bloat (gastric torsion): Life‑threatening stomach twist. Signs: unproductive vomiting (dry heaving), distended belly, restlessness. Emergency! Feed two small meals daily, no exercise after eating.
  • Cancer: Rottweilers have higher risk of bone cancer (osteosarcoma). Any limp that doesn’t go away needs X‑rays.
  • Heart disease: Subvalvular aortic stenosis (SAS) – a heart defect. Regular heart checks recommended.
  • Eye problems: Cataracts and progressive retinal atrophy (PRA).

Grooming – easy

  • Brush once a week with a rubber curry brush or soft bristle brush – removes loose hair and spreads skin oils.
  • They shed moderately, more in spring and autumn – brush twice a week during shedding season.
  • Bathe once a month or when dirty – use a mild dog shampoo.
  • Clean ears weekly with a cotton ball – never Q‑tips.
  • Trim nails every 3–4 weeks. Brush teeth weekly.

Exercise needs

Rottweilers are active but not hyper. They need daily exercise to stay calm.

  • Walks: 30–45 minutes twice a day. Avoid peak heat – use the paw test (Week 13).
  • Mental stimulation: Training sessions, puzzle toys, nose work – they are smart and need a job.
  • Secure yard: They need space to move, but the fence must be strong – they are powerful.
  • Avoid over‑exercise as puppies: Too much running on hard surfaces can damage growing joints.

Training and behaviour – very important

  • Start training and socialisation as a puppy – expose them to many people, dogs, and situations.
  • Use positive reinforcement – Rottweilers respond well to treats and praise. Harsh methods can make them fearful or aggressive.
  • They need a confident owner who sets clear rules. They are not for first‑time dog owners.
  • They are natural protectors. Teach them the difference between a normal visitor and a real threat – otherwise, they may become over‑protective.

Feeding guidelines

  • High‑quality large breed dog food – they need good nutrition for their joints.
  • For an adult Rottweiler, 3–5 cups of dry food per day (divided into two meals). Adjust based on activity and weight.
  • Do not feed one large meal – this increases bloat risk. Divide into two or three small meals.
  • Keep them lean – extra weight stresses joints and worsens dysplasia.
  • Always fresh, clean water – but restrict water for 1 hour after meals to prevent bloat.

Real story from our clinic

A 4‑year‑old Rottweiler named “Rocky” came in with restlessness and a swollen belly. He was trying to vomit but nothing came out – classic signs of bloat. We rushed him to surgery. His stomach had twisted. We untwisted it and tacked it in place to prevent recurrence. Rocky survived, but the surgery was intense. His owner now feeds him two small meals a day and never exercises him after eating. “I had no idea bloat could kill so fast,” he said. “Now I am very careful.”

Should you get a Rottweiler?

Yes, if you:

  • Are an experienced dog owner – not your first dog.
  • Have time for training, socialisation, and daily exercise.
  • Have a secure yard with strong fencing.
  • Can commit to feeding two small meals daily and watching for bloat signs.

Maybe not, if you:

  • Are a first‑time owner – Rottweilers need a confident leader.
  • Do not have time for daily exercise – they become destructive.
  • Have young children and are not able to supervise interactions (though Rottweilers can be good with family, they are large and can knock over small kids).
  • Live in an apartment with no secure outdoor space.

Action for Week 17

  1. If you have a Rottweiler, check their hips – do they have difficulty getting up or limping? Schedule a hip X‑ray.
  2. Change to two small meals a day if you are feeding one large meal – reduces bloat risk.
  3. Start a daily training session – even 5 minutes of obedience work keeps their mind sharp.
  4. If you are thinking of getting a Rottweiler, visit a reputable breeder who tests for hip dysplasia and heart disease.
