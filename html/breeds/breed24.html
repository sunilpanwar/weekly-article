Week 24 • Cat Breed

🐈 Burmese Cat – Playful, Social, Prone to Diabetes and Cranial Issues

The Burmese cat is a medium‑sized, muscular cat with a sleek, shiny coat and large, expressive eyes. They are known for being very people‑oriented – they will follow you around, talk to you, and demand attention. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see Burmese cats that are playful, affectionate, and great with families. But they also have some health problems. They are prone to diabetes, especially if overweight. They can also have a condition called cranial deformities (flat face issues similar to Persians). They are indoor cats and need plenty of interaction.

🐾 Key things to remember: Burmese cats love to eat – measure every meal to prevent obesity and diabetes. They need daily play and attention – they do not like being alone. Their slightly flat face can cause breathing and dental issues.

What does a Burmese cat look like?

  • Size: Small to medium – 3–6 kg. Surprising weight for their size – they are muscular and heavy.
  • Coat: Short, fine, and shiny – like satin. Very low grooming.
  • Colours: Sable (rich brown), champagne (warm beige), blue (dilute grey), platinum (pale silver).
  • Build: Round head, large round eyes (gold to yellow), short muzzle, and medium ears.

Why Burmese cats are popular in India

  • Very affectionate: They are often called “velcro cats” because they stick to their owners.
  • Playful and intelligent: They can learn tricks and love interactive toys.
  • Low grooming: Short coat needs only weekly brushing.
  • Good with children and dogs: They are social and confident.
🍬 Dr. Deepa’s warning: “Burmese cats have a high risk of diabetes, especially if they are overweight. Do not free‑feed. Measure portions and avoid sugary treats. Also, their slightly flat face can cause dental crowding – brush their teeth weekly.”

Common health problems in Burmese cats

  • Diabetes mellitus: Common in Burmese, especially overweight cats. Signs: increased thirst, increased urination, weight loss. Diet control is important.
  • Cranial deformities (brachycephalic traits): Their short muzzle can cause breathing issues (snoring, heat intolerance) and dental overcrowding.
  • Dental disease: Crowded teeth trap food, leading to tartar and gingivitis. Brush weekly.
  • Hypokalemia (low potassium): A genetic muscle weakness condition. Blood test can diagnose.
  • Obesity: They love to eat – measure portions strictly.

Grooming – easy

  • Brush once a week with a soft rubber brush – removes loose hair and makes the coat shine.
  • Bathe rarely – only when dirty. Their coat is naturally glossy.
  • Clean ears weekly with a cotton ball – never Q‑tips.
  • Trim nails every 2–3 weeks.
  • Brush teeth weekly – very important for dental health.

Exercise and play – very important

Burmese cats are active and social. They need daily interaction to prevent boredom and obesity.

  • Play sessions: 15–20 minutes twice a day – feather wands, fetch, puzzle toys.
  • Climbing: Cat trees or shelves – they like to be up high.
  • Companionship: They do best with another cat or a dog – they hate being alone.

Feeding guidelines – very important

  • High‑quality cat food – low in carbohydrates to reduce diabetes risk.
  • For an adult Burmese, 1/4 to 1/3 cup of dry food per day (divided into two meals) plus wet food.
  • Do not free‑feed (leave food out). They will overeat and become obese.
  • Avoid high‑sugar treats – use small pieces of cooked chicken or fish.
  • Always fresh, clean water.

Real story from our clinic

A Burmese cat named “Mocha” came in for excessive thirst and weight loss. Blood tests showed diabetes. He was overweight from free‑feeding dry food. We put him on a strict diet – measured low‑carbohydrate wet food twice daily. He also started insulin injections. Within a month, his blood sugar improved. His owner now measures every meal and says, “I didn’t know Burmese are prone to diabetes. Now I watch his weight very carefully.”

Should you get a Burmese cat?

Yes, if you:

  • Have time for daily play and attention – they need interaction.
  • Can commit to strict portion control – they will overeat if allowed.
  • Are home often – they do not like being left alone.
  • Can brush their teeth weekly and monitor for diabetes signs.

Maybe not, if you:

  • Work long hours and cannot give them attention – they get lonely and destructive.
  • Cannot resist giving extra food – obesity and diabetes are real risks.
  • Want a low‑maintenance, independent cat (get an Indie cat instead).

Action for Week 24

  1. If you have a Burmese, check their weight – feel their ribs. If you can’t feel them easily, reduce food.
  2. Watch for increased thirst or urination – these are early diabetes signs. Bring a urine sample to us.
  3. Start a weekly tooth‑brushing routine – use cat‑safe toothpaste.
  4. If you are thinking of getting a Burmese, adopt from a rescue or ask the breeder about diabetes history in the line.
