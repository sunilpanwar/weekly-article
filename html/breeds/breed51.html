Week 51 • Dog Breed

🐕 Corgi – Short Legs, Long Body, Prone to Back Problems and Obesity

The Corgi (Pembroke or Cardigan) is a small, sturdy herding dog with short legs, a long body, and a big personality. They are famous for their foxy face, large ears, and adorable “sploot” (lying with back legs stretched out). At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see Corgis that are intelligent, playful, and very loyal. However, their long spine puts them at high risk for back problems (intervertebral disc disease – IVDD). They also love to eat and become obese very easily – extra weight is terrible for their back. They were bred to herd cattle, so they can be bossy and may nip at heels. They are great for active families but need careful weight management and back protection.

🐾 Key things to remember: Corgis have a long back – never let them jump off furniture (use ramps). Keep them lean – every extra gram stresses their spine. They are herders – may nip at children’s heels. They shed heavily – brush 2–3 times a week.

What does a Corgi look like?

  • Size: Small to medium – 10–14 kg. Height 25–30 cm.
  • Coat: Thick, double coat – colours: red, sable, fawn, black and tan, with or without white markings.
  • Build: Long body, very short legs, deep chest, large erect ears, and no tail (Pembroke) or long tail (Cardigan).

Why Corgis are popular – and have special needs

  • Big personality: They are smart, confident, and often funny – great companions.
  • Energetic: They need daily exercise and mental stimulation.
  • Back problems: Their long spine makes them prone to slipped discs (IVDD).
  • Heavy shedders: They shed year‑round, more in spring and autumn.
🦴 Dr. Deepa’s warning: “Corgis have a very high risk of intervertebral disc disease (IVDD). Never let them jump off sofas or beds – use ramps. Keep them lean – obesity is the number one cause of back problems in Corgis. Also, they are herders – they may chase and nip at children’s heels. Train them not to do this from puppyhood.”

Common health problems in Corgis

  • Intervertebral disc disease (IVDD): Back problems. Signs: crying when picked up, hunched back, wobbly walk, dragging legs. Emergency! Rest, medication, or surgery may be needed.
  • Obesity: Very common – extra weight stresses the long spine. Measure every meal.
  • Hip dysplasia: Keep them lean.
  • Eye problems: Progressive retinal atrophy (PRA) – yearly eye exams recommended.
  • Degenerative myelopathy: A spinal cord disease – rare but possible.

Grooming – moderate to high

  • Brush 2–3 times a week: Use a slicker brush and undercoat rake. During shedding season (spring, autumn), brush daily – they “blow” their coat.
  • Bathe once a month or when dirty – use a mild dog shampoo. Dry thoroughly.
  • Clean ears weekly – their large erect ears can trap dirt.
  • Trim nails every 3–4 weeks. Brush teeth weekly.

Exercise and back protection – very important

  • Walks: 20–30 minutes twice a day. Avoid jumping, running down stairs, or rough play that twists their back.
  • Ramps: Use ramps or stairs for sofas and beds – never let them jump down.
  • No pulling on collar: Use a harness (not a neck collar) to protect their spine.
  • Mental stimulation: They are smart – need puzzle toys, training, and herding games (like chasing a ball).

Training and behaviour

  • Corgis are very intelligent but can be stubborn. Use positive reinforcement (treats, praise).
  • They have a strong herding instinct – may chase and nip at children’s heels or other pets. Train “leave it” early.
  • They bark a lot – train a “quiet” command.
  • Early socialisation is important – they can be wary of strangers.

Feeding guidelines

  • High‑quality dog food – measure portions to prevent obesity.
  • For an adult Corgi, 1–1.5 cups of dry food per day (divided into two meals). Adjust based on activity.
  • Keep them lean – you should easily feel their ribs.
  • Limit treats – use small pieces of carrot or apple.
  • Always fresh, clean water.

Real story from our clinic

A Corgi named “Ein” came in with a hunched back and crying when touched. He had jumped off the sofa and slipped a disc. X‑rays confirmed IVDD. He needed surgery and weeks of crate rest. After recovery, his owner installed ramps and stopped letting him jump. “I didn’t know a small jump could cause so much damage,” she said. “Now we are very careful.”

Should you get a Corgi?

Yes, if you:

  • Can use ramps and prevent jumping – no sofas or beds without ramps.
  • Can commit to keeping them lean – no overfeeding.
  • Have time for daily walks and mental stimulation.
  • Are prepared for heavy shedding and potential back problem treatment costs.

Maybe not, if you:

  • Live in a home with high furniture and cannot use ramps – jumping is dangerous.
  • Cannot resist giving extra food – obesity will damage their back.
  • Have very young children who may not respect the dog’s space – Corgis may nip heels.
  • Want a low‑shedding, quiet dog – Corgis shed a lot and bark.

Action for Week 51

  1. If you have a Corgi, check their back – any signs of pain or weakness? Bring them immediately.
  2. Install ramps or steps for sofas and beds – prevent jumping.
  3. Switch to a harness if you use a neck collar – protects the spine.
  4. If you are thinking of getting a Corgi, adopt from a rescue or ask the breeder about back health and eye screening.
