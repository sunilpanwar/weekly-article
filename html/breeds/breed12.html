Week 12 • Cat Breed

🐱 Ragdoll Cat – Gentle, Floppy, and Prone to Bladder Stones

The Ragdoll cat is known for going limp like a ragdoll when you pick them up. They are large, fluffy, and very gentle. They love to be held, cuddled, and carried around – unlike many independent cats. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see Ragdolls that are wonderful family pets. But they also have some health problems – especially bladder stones and a heart condition called HCM. They are indoor cats only – they are too trusting to survive outside.

🐾 Key things to remember: Ragdolls go limp when held – they love cuddles. They are strictly indoor cats – they have no road sense and will not fight. They need regular brushing and are prone to bladder stones – provide plenty of water.

What does a Ragdoll cat look like?

  • Size: Large – 4–9 kg. Females smaller, males can be very big.
  • Coat: Semi‑long, soft, and silky – not as thick as a Persian. Low matting if brushed weekly.
  • Colours: Colour‑point – lighter body with darker ears, face, paws, and tail. Seal, blue, chocolate, lilac, or red.
  • Eyes: Large, round, deep blue.
  • Build: Muscular, heavy, with a soft belly. They feel heavier than they look.

Why Ragdolls are popular in India

  • Gentle temperament: They are calm, patient, and good with children – they rarely scratch or bite.
  • Affectionate: They follow their owners around and love to be held. Good for people who want a “lap cat”.
  • Dog‑like behaviour: They greet you at the door, come when called, and can learn tricks.
  • Quiet: They are not very vocal – they meow softly, not loudly like Siamese.
💎 Dr. Deepa’s warning: “Ragdolls are prone to bladder stones (calcium oxalate and struvite). Feed them wet food to increase water intake. Also, never let them outside – they are too gentle and will not survive.”

Common health problems in Ragdolls

  • Bladder stones (urolithiasis): Very common. Signs: straining to pee, blood in urine, peeing outside the litter box. Wet food diet and plenty of water help prevent.
  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM): A heart disease that thickens the heart muscle. Yearly heart screening is recommended.
  • Obesity: Ragdolls love to eat and can become overweight – measure portions.
  • Dental disease: Brush their teeth weekly.
  • Feline infectious peritonitis (FIP): A viral disease more common in purebred cats – get from a responsible breeder.

Grooming – moderate

  • Brush 2–3 times a week: Use a steel comb and a soft slicker brush. Their coat does not mat easily, but regular brushing prevents tangles.
  • During shedding season (spring/autumn): Brush daily – they shed a lot for a longhair.
  • Bathe once a month or when needed – Ragdolls are clean, but their white paws can get dirty.
  • Clean ears weekly with a cotton ball – never Q‑tips.
  • Trim nails every 2–3 weeks. Brush teeth weekly.

Exercise and environment

Ragdolls are not as active as Bengals, but they still need play and climbing space.

  • Play: 15–20 minutes twice a day – feather wands, fetch, puzzle toys.
  • Climbing: Cat trees or shelves – they like to be up high but are not extreme jumpers.
  • Indoor only: Never let a Ragdoll outside alone. They are too trusting and may be attacked by dogs or stolen.
  • Leash training: Some Ragdolls can be walked on a harness – supervised outdoor time is safe.

Feeding guidelines

  • Feed wet food as the main diet – very important for bladder health. Dry food alone increases stone risk.
  • For an adult Ragdoll, one 80–100g can of wet food twice a day, plus a small amount of dry (1/4 cup).
  • Measure portions – they can become overweight.
  • Always fresh water – use a cat water fountain to encourage drinking.
  • Avoid high‑magnesium foods – they can promote struvite stones. Ask us for a diet recommendation.

Real story from our clinic

A 4‑year‑old Ragdoll named “Fluffy” was brought in because he was straining to pee and crying. His bladder was full of small stones. We performed surgery to remove the stones. Fluffy recovered, but his owner learned that feeding only dry food caused the problem. Now Fluffy gets wet food twice a day and drinks from a fountain. The owner says, “I had no idea dry food could cause stones. Now I tell all Ragdoll owners to feed wet food.”

Should you get a Ragdoll cat?

Yes, if you:

  • Want a gentle, cuddly, affectionate cat.
  • Have a safe, indoor‑only home – no letting them outside.
  • Can feed wet food (more expensive than dry) to prevent bladder stones.
  • Are ready for regular brushing (2–3 times a week) and yearly heart checks.

Maybe not, if you:

  • Want an independent, low‑maintenance cat (Ragdolls need attention).
  • Cannot afford wet food or potential bladder stone treatment.
  • Have a home where doors are often left open – they may escape and get lost.
  • Want a cat that can go outside – Ragdolls must be indoor only.

Action for Week 12

  1. If you have a Ragdoll, check their litter box – is there blood or are they straining? Any change in peeing habits needs a vet visit.
  2. Increase water intake – add a water fountain or mix extra water into their wet food.
  3. Brush their coat today – check for mats behind the ears and under the armpits.
  4. If you are thinking of getting a Ragdoll, ask the breeder about HCM screening in the parents.
