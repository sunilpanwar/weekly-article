🐕 Greyhound – Gentle, Fast, but Has Thin Skin and Needs Soft Bedding

The Greyhound is famous for being the fastest dog breed – they can run up to 70 km per hour. But off the track, they are calm, gentle, and love to lounge. They are often called “45‑mph couch potatoes”. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see Greyhounds that are sweet, quiet, and good with families. However, they have some special needs. Their skin is very thin and tears easily. They have very little body fat, so they need soft bedding and warm clothes in winter. They also have a high risk of dental disease and bloat. They are not for apartments unless you have a secure yard for running.

🐾 Key things to remember: Greyhounds have thin skin – a small scratch can become a large tear. They need soft, padded beds (hard floors cause pressure sores). They are prone to dental disease – brush teeth weekly. Never let them off‑leash in an unsecured area – they will chase and not return.

What does a Greyhound look like?

  • Size: Large – 25–40 kg. Height 65–75 cm.
  • Coat: Short, smooth – many colours: black, white, brindle, fawn, blue, red.
  • Build: Lean, deep chest, long legs, curved tail, and a long, narrow head.

Why Greyhounds are special – and have special needs

  • Gentle temperament: They are quiet, calm, and good with children – despite their racing reputation.
  • Low energy indoors: They love to sleep on the sofa – perfect for calm homes.
  • Thin skin: Their skin is like paper – a small bump can cause a tear. They need careful handling.
  • Cold sensitive: Very little body fat – they need sweaters in winter and soft, warm beds.
🩹 Dr. Deepa’s warning: “Greyhounds have very thin skin. A scratch from a cat or a bump against a sharp corner can cause a flap tear that needs stitches. Check your home for sharp edges. Also, never let them off‑leash outside a secure fenced area – their prey drive is huge, and they will chase small animals.”

Common health problems in Greyhounds

  • Skin injuries (lacerations, abrasions): Very common. Any cut should be checked – they often need stitches.
  • Dental disease (periodontitis): Very common. Brush teeth weekly and get yearly cleanings.
  • Bloat (gastric torsion): Deep chest increases risk – feed two small meals daily, no exercise after eating.
  • Bone cancer (osteosarcoma): More common in Greyhounds than many breeds – any limp needs X‑rays.
  • Pressure sores (calluses): From lying on hard surfaces – provide thick, soft bedding.

Grooming – very easy

  • Brush once a week with a rubber curry brush – removes loose hair and spreads skin oils.
  • Bathe once a month or when dirty – use a mild shampoo. Be gentle – their skin is thin.
  • Clean ears weekly – their ears are prone to dirt.
  • Trim nails every 3–4 weeks – long nails can split and cause pain.
  • Brush teeth weekly – very important.

Exercise and environment

  • Running space: A securely fenced yard (minimum 6‑foot height) is ideal. They need short bursts of running, not long walks.
  • Leash walking: Always on a leash outside a secure yard – they will chase and not recall.
  • Soft bedding: Orthopaedic or thick foam beds – hard floors cause painful calluses.
  • Winter care: Use sweaters or coats when it is cold – they have no body fat.

Training and behaviour

  • Greyhounds are sensitive and gentle. Use positive reinforcement – harsh methods will shut them down.
  • They can be difficult to house‑train – be patient and consistent.
  • They are quiet – they rarely bark. Not good watchdogs.
  • Many retired racers are not used to stairs, glass doors, or mirrors – introduce them slowly.

Feeding guidelines

  • High‑quality large breed dog food – they need good nutrition but are prone to bloat.
  • For an adult Greyhound, 2–4 cups of dry food per day (divided into two meals). Adjust based on activity.
  • Do not feed one large meal – feed two small meals to prevent bloat.
  • Always fresh, clean water – but restrict water for 1 hour after meals.

Real story from our clinic

A Greyhound named “Flash” came in with a deep gash on his leg from bumping into a metal garden edging. He needed 12 stitches. His owner then padded all sharp edges in the yard. “I had no idea their skin is so fragile,” she said. “Now we are very careful.”

Should you get a Greyhound?

Yes, if you:

  • Have a securely fenced yard (minimum 6‑foot fence).
  • Can provide soft bedding and protect sharp edges.
  • Are prepared for potential dental care and bloat prevention.
  • Want a calm, gentle, low‑energy indoor dog (once exercised).

Maybe not, if you:

  • Have a small, unsecured yard – they will escape.
  • Have small pets (cats, rabbits) – their prey drive is very high.
  • Live in a very cold climate without warm clothing for the dog.
  • Are not prepared for thin‑skin injuries and vet costs.

Action for Week 44

  1. If you have a Greyhound, check your home for sharp edges – pad them.
  2. Provide a thick, orthopaedic bed – no hard floors.
  3. Schedule a dental check – Greyhounds have bad teeth.
  4. If you are thinking of getting a Greyhound, adopt a retired racer from a rescue group.
