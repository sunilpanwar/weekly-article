Week 21 • Dog Breed

🐕 Boxer – Energetic, Playful, but Prone to Cancer and Heart Problems

The Boxer is a medium‑sized, muscular dog with a playful, energetic personality. They are known for being goofy, loyal, and great with children. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see Boxers that are full of life – they stay playful even as adults. But they also have serious health problems. Boxers have a high risk of cancer, especially mast cell tumours. They also have a heart condition called aortic stenosis. They are sensitive to heat because of their flat face. If you want a Boxer, you must be ready for regular vet checks and a lot of exercise.

🐾 Key things to remember: Boxers have a high cancer risk – check their skin regularly for lumps. They are very energetic and need 1+ hour of exercise daily. Their flat face makes them sensitive to heat – walk only early morning or late evening.

What does a Boxer look like?

  • Size: Medium‑large – males 30–35 kg, females 25–30 kg. Height 55–65 cm.
  • Coat: Short, smooth, and shiny – very low grooming.
  • Colours: Fawn (tan) or brindle (striped), with or without white markings.
  • Build: Muscular, square‑shaped body, strong jaw, floppy ears (often cropped in some countries, but natural in India), and a short, flat face.

Why Boxers are popular in India

  • Playful and energetic: They are great for active families.
  • Loyal and protective: They bond closely with their family and are good watchdogs.
  • Good with children: Boxers are famously patient and gentle with kids.
  • Low grooming: Short coat needs only weekly brushing.
🩺 Dr. Deepa’s warning: “Boxers have a very high cancer rate – up to 40% will develop cancer, especially mast cell tumours. Check your Boxer’s skin every week. Any lump that grows, changes colour, or itches needs immediate vet attention.”

Common health problems in Boxers

  • Cancer (mast cell tumours, lymphoma, brain tumours): Very common. Weekly skin checks are essential. Early removal saves lives.
  • Aortic stenosis (subvalvular): A heart defect that can cause fainting or sudden death. Yearly heart checks (echocardiogram) recommended.
  • Brachycephalic airway syndrome: Flat face causes snoring, wheezing, and heat intolerance. Avoid hot weather.
  • Bloat (gastric torsion): Life‑threatening stomach twist. Feed two small meals daily, no exercise after eating.
  • Hip dysplasia: Common in large breeds. Keep them lean.

Grooming – very easy

  • Brush once a week with a rubber curry brush – removes loose hair and spreads skin oils.
  • Bathe once a month or when dirty – use a mild dog shampoo.
  • Clean ears weekly – their ears can get dirty if floppy.
  • Trim nails every 3–4 weeks. Brush teeth weekly.

Exercise needs – high

Boxers are very energetic and need daily vigorous exercise. A bored Boxer can become destructive.

  • Walks: 30–45 minutes twice a day. Avoid peak heat – use the paw test (Week 13).
  • Running and fetch: They love to run – a fenced yard is ideal.
  • Mental stimulation: They are smart and need training, puzzle toys, and games. A tired mind is as important as a tired body.
  • Secure fencing: Boxers can jump high – your fence must be tall and strong.

Training and behaviour

  • Boxers are smart but can be stubborn. Use positive reinforcement (treats, praise).
  • Start training and socialisation as puppies – they grow into well‑behaved adults.
  • They are very playful and may jump on people – teach them not to jump early.
  • They can develop separation anxiety if left alone for long hours – they want to be with their family.

Feeding guidelines

  • High‑quality dog food – they need good nutrition for their energy levels.
  • For an adult Boxer, 2.5–3.5 cups of dry food per day (divided into two meals). Adjust based on activity and weight.
  • Do not overfeed – keep them lean to reduce cancer and joint risks.
  • Limit treats – use small pieces of apple or carrot.
  • Always fresh, clean water.

Real story from our clinic

A 6‑year‑old Boxer named “Rocky” came in for a checkup. His owner had noticed a small lump on his leg that had grown over a month. We biopsied it – it was a mast cell tumour. We removed it surgically with clean margins. Rocky recovered well. His owner now checks his skin every week. “I almost ignored the lump,” he said. “Now I know Boxers get cancer often. Early removal saved his life.”

Should you get a Boxer?

Yes, if you:

  • Have time for daily exercise (1+ hours) and mental stimulation.
  • Can commit to regular skin checks for lumps and yearly heart screening.
  • Live in a cool home or have air conditioning in summer – they overheat easily.
  • Want an energetic, playful, family dog.

Maybe not, if you:

  • Work long hours and cannot give them attention – they get anxious and destructive.
  • Live in a very hot, humid city without AC.
  • Cannot afford regular vet visits for cancer and heart screening.
  • Want a calm, low‑energy dog – Boxers are very active.

Action for Week 21

  1. If you have a Boxer, feel their entire body for lumps today – check between toes, under armpits, and around the neck.
  2. Schedule a heart screening (echocardiogram) if your Boxer is over 2 years old.
  3. Start daily exercise if you have been skipping – a tired Boxer is a well‑behaved Boxer.
  4. If you are thinking of getting a Boxer, adopt from a rescue or ask the breeder about heart and cancer history in the parents.
