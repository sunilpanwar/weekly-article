Week 25 • Dog Breed

🐕 Dachshund – Long Body, Short Legs, Prone to Back Problems

The Dachshund, also called the “wiener dog”, has a long body, short legs, and a big personality. They were bred to hunt badgers – so they are brave, curious, and sometimes stubborn. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see Dachshunds that are loving family pets. But their long spine makes them prone to serious back problems (intervertebral disc disease – IVDD). They can become paralysed if they jump off furniture or become overweight. They also love to dig and bark. They are good for apartments but need careful handling.

🐾 Key things to remember: Dachshunds should not jump off sofas or beds – use ramps. Keep them lean – every extra gram stresses their long spine. They are prone to back problems – if they show pain or weakness, see the vet immediately.

What does a Dachshund look like?

  • Size: Small – standard (7–14 kg) or miniature (under 5 kg).
  • Coat: Three types – smooth (short), longhaired, or wirehaired.
  • Colours: Red, black and tan, chocolate, dapple (spotted), or piebald.
  • Build: Long body, very short legs, deep chest, long tail, and floppy ears.

Why Dachshunds are popular in India

  • Playful and brave: They have a big dog attitude in a small body.
  • Good for apartments: Small size fits most homes.
  • Affectionate: They bond closely with their family.
  • Variety: Three coat types to choose from.
🦴 Dr. Deepa’s warning: “Dachshunds have a very high risk of intervertebral disc disease (IVDD) – the discs between their spine bones can slip or rupture. This can cause pain, weakness, or even paralysis. Never let them jump off furniture. Use ramps. Keep them lean – obesity is very bad for their back.”

Common health problems in Dachshunds

  • Intervertebral disc disease (IVDD): Back problems. Signs: crying when picked up, hunched back, wobbly walk, dragging back legs. Emergency! Rest, medication, or surgery may be needed.
  • Obesity: Extra weight puts huge stress on the long spine. Measure every meal.
  • Dental disease: Small mouth = crowded teeth. Brush weekly.
  • Patellar luxation: Loose kneecaps – can cause limping.
  • Bloat (gastric torsion): Less common in small dogs, but possible in standard Dachshunds with deep chests.

Grooming – depends on coat type

  • Smooth coat: Brush once a week with a soft brush – remove loose hair.
  • Longhair: Brush 2–3 times a week to prevent mats.
  • Wirehair: Brush weekly and hand‑strip (pluck dead hair) a few times a year.
  • Bathe once a month or when dirty.
  • Clean ears weekly – their floppy ears trap moisture.
  • Trim nails every 3–4 weeks. Brush teeth weekly.

Exercise and back protection – very important

  • Walks: 20–30 minutes twice a day. Avoid jumping, running down stairs, or rough play that twists their back.
  • Ramps: Use ramps or stairs for sofas and beds – never let them jump down.
  • No pulling on collar: Use a harness (not a neck collar) to protect their spine.
  • Secure yard: Dachshunds love to dig – they can escape under fences.

Training and behaviour

  • Dachshunds are smart but stubborn. Use positive reinforcement (treats, praise).
  • They bark a lot – train a “quiet” command early.
  • They love to dig – give them a sandbox or designated digging spot.
  • They can be aggressive with strange dogs if not socialised – start early.

Feeding guidelines

  • High‑quality small breed dog food – measure portions to prevent obesity.
  • For an adult Dachshund, 1/2 to 1 cup of dry food per day (divided into two meals) – adjust based on size and activity.
  • Keep them lean – you should easily feel their ribs.
  • Limit treats – use small pieces of carrot or apple.
  • Always fresh, clean water.

Real story from our clinic

A 5‑year‑old miniature Dachshund named “Oscar” was brought in because he was crying when picked up and walking with a hunched back. He had jumped off the sofa the day before. X‑rays showed a slipped disc. He needed surgery to remove the disc material. After surgery and weeks of rest, Oscar recovered. His owner now uses a ramp for the sofa and says, “I never thought a small jump could cause so much damage. Now we are very careful.”

Should you get a Dachshund?

Yes, if you:

  • Can use ramps and prevent jumping – no sofas or beds without ramps.
  • Can commit to keeping them lean – no overfeeding.
  • Have time for daily walks and play.
  • Are prepared for potential back problem treatment costs.

Maybe not, if you:

  • Live in a home with high furniture and cannot use ramps – jumping is dangerous.
  • Cannot resist giving extra food – obesity will damage their back.
  • Want a quiet, low‑barking dog – Dachshunds bark a lot.
  • Have small children who may handle the dog roughly (back injury risk).

Action for Week 25

  1. If you have a Dachshund, check their back – do they show pain when picked up? Do they walk normally? Any signs of weakness? Bring them immediately.
  2. Install ramps or steps for sofas and beds – prevent jumping.
  3. Switch to a harness if you use a neck collar – protects the spine.
  4. If you are thinking of getting a Dachshund, adopt from a rescue or ask the breeder about back health in the parents.
