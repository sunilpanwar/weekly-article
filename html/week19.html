✂️ The Grooming Myth – Shaving a Labrador Won’t Make Them Cooler

Every summer, pet parents bring in double‑coated dogs like Labradors, Golden Retrievers, German Shepherds, and Indies – completely shaved. They believe it helps their pet stay cool. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we want to set the record straight: shaving a double‑coated dog is harmful, not helpful. It removes their natural insulation and exposes their skin to sunburn, heatstroke, and skin cancer.

✂️ Common sense rule: Never shave a double‑coated dog. Their coat insulates against both heat and cold. Instead, brush daily to remove loose undercoat and allow air circulation.

How a double coat works – the science

Double‑coated breeds have two layers:

  • Undercoat: Soft, dense, fluffy fur that traps air – insulating against heat and cold.
  • Guard coat: Longer, coarse outer hairs that reflect sunlight and repel moisture.

In summer, the undercoat sheds out, leaving the guard coat to reflect heat. The trapped air layer actually keeps the dog cooler than being bare‑skinned. Shaving removes both layers, destroying this natural cooling system.

Why shaving is dangerous for double‑coated dogs

  • Sunburn: The guard coat protects against UV rays. Shaved skin burns easily, leading to pain and increased risk of skin cancer.
  • Heatstroke risk increases: Without the insulating air layer, the dog absorbs more heat directly.
  • Permanent coat damage: The undercoat may grow back thicker, and the guard coat may never regrow properly, leading to patchy, uneven fur.
  • Irritation and itching: Regrowing hair can be itchy, and the dog may scratch excessively, causing skin infections.
☀️ Dr. Deepa’s warning: “I’ve seen dogs with severe sunburn on their backs after being shaved. They whine when touched and need weeks of treatment. A brush is safer than clippers.”

Which breeds should NEVER be shaved?

  • Labrador Retrievers
  • Golden Retrievers
  • German Shepherds
  • Huskies and Malamutes
  • Indies (Indian Pariah dogs)
  • Chow Chows
  • Pomeranians
  • Akitas

These breeds have double coats designed for temperature regulation. Shaving them is never recommended except for medical reasons (surgery, severe matting that can’t be brushed out).

What TO do instead – summer grooming for double coats

  • Brush daily with an undercoat rake or deshedding tool. This removes loose undercoat, allowing air to circulate.
  • Bathe with a deshedding shampoo – follow with a blow‑dry on cool setting while brushing outdoors. Hair will fly off in clouds.
  • Trim paw pads – long fur between toes traps heat and makes walking uncomfortable. Use blunt scissors to trim flush with the pad.
  • Provide plenty of water and shade – even with a healthy coat, summer heat is dangerous.

What about single‑coated breeds or cats?

Breeds with continuously growing hair (Poodles, Shih Tzus, Bichon Frises, some terriers) can have a summer trim – a shorter cut (not shaved to the skin) can help. But even then, leaving at least 1 inch of fur protects from sun. Cats generally should not be shaved except for medical reasons – they rely on their coat for temperature regulation and protection.

Real case from our clinic

A 3‑year‑old Labrador, Bruno, was shaved by a groomer in April. The owner thought it would help him stay cool. Within a week, Bruno’s back was red, swollen, and painful. He was constantly scratching. We diagnosed sunburn and skin irritation from regrowing hair. He needed medicated shampoos, antibiotics for secondary infection, and a cone to stop licking. The owner spent more on treatment than the grooming cost. Bruno’s coat grew back patchy and never fully recovered. The owner now tells everyone: “Never shave a Lab.”

When is shaving medically necessary?

There are rare cases where a vet may recommend shaving:

  • Severe matting that cannot be brushed out (painful and traps moisture).
  • Before surgery or ultrasound.
  • Skin conditions requiring topical treatment.
  • Severe heatstroke treatment (only in emergency).

In these cases, the benefits outweigh the risks – but it’s always a veterinary decision, not a grooming preference.

How to keep your double‑coated dog cool in summer

  • Walk early morning (before 7 AM) and late evening.
  • Provide a cooling mat or a damp towel for them to lie on.
  • Fill a kiddie pool with cool water for supervised splashing.
  • Use a fan or air conditioning indoors.
  • Brush, brush, brush – daily brushing removes the thick undercoat that traps heat.

Action for Week 19

  1. Check your dog’s coat type – is it double or single? If double, never shave.
  2. Buy an undercoat rake or deshedding tool. Brush outdoors for 5 minutes daily.
  3. If your dog is already shaved, keep them indoors and apply pet‑safe sunscreen (zinc oxide is toxic – ask us for recommendations).
  4. Share this information with your groomer – many still shave double coats out of habit.
