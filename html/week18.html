🚨 Heatstroke Signs – Recognise and Respond to Overheating

Heatstroke kills pets in minutes. It’s not just “getting too hot” – it’s a medical emergency where the body’s cooling system fails, causing organ damage and death. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see preventable heatstroke cases every summer. Knowing the early signs and acting fast can save your pet’s life.

🚨 Common sense rule: If your pet is panting excessively and their tongue is dark red, they are overheating. Apply cool (not cold) wet towels to their belly and paws, and get to a vet immediately.

The three stages of heat illness

  • Heat stress (early stage): Heavy panting, restless, seeking shade or water. Body temperature 39–40°C (102–104°F). Act now – cooling can reverse it.
  • Heat exhaustion (moderate stage): Excessive drooling, dark red gums, weakness, vomiting. Body temperature 40–41°C (104–106°F). Emergency – vet visit required.
  • Heatstroke (severe stage): Collapse, seizures, bloody diarrhoea, unconsciousness. Body temperature >41°C (>106°F). Critical – death is imminent without aggressive treatment.

Early warning signs – don’t ignore these

  • Panting that is louder or faster than usual, even after resting.
  • Tongue and gums that are bright red or dark purple.
  • Thick, ropey saliva or drooling.
  • Restlessness or agitation – unable to settle.
  • Seeking cool surfaces (tiles, shade) but not improving.

Moderate to severe signs – act immediately

  • Weakness, stumbling, or inability to stand.
  • Vomiting or diarrhoea (may contain blood).
  • Glazed eyes or disorientation.
  • Seizures or muscle tremors.
  • Collapse or loss of consciousness.
🌡️ Dr. Deepa’s warning: “If your pet’s temperature is above 104°F (40°C), start cooling immediately. But do not try to take a rectal temperature if your pet is distressed – you may get bitten. Focus on cooling and transport to the clinic.”

What to do if you suspect heatstroke – step by step

  1. Move your pet to a cool, shaded, or air‑conditioned area. Stop all activity.
  2. Apply cool (not ice‑cold) water to the belly, armpits, and paw pads. Use a hose, wet towels, or pour water. Do not immerse in cold water – that can cause shock.
  3. Use fans to increase evaporative cooling.
  4. Offer small sips of cool water – do not force. If they won’t drink, wet their tongue.
  5. Do NOT use ice or ice water. Ice constricts blood vessels, trapping heat inside.
  6. Transport to the clinic immediately – call ahead if possible so we can prepare.

What NOT to do in a heatstroke emergency

  • Do not wait to see if they improve. Heatstroke worsens rapidly. Every minute counts.
  • Do not put your pet in a cold bath or cover with ice packs. This causes shock and slows cooling.
  • Do not leave them unattended. They may collapse or have seizures.
  • Do not give human fever medication (paracetamol, aspirin). It is toxic to pets.

Real case from our clinic

A 7‑year‑old Pug, Golu, was brought in after a 15‑minute walk at 10 AM. He collapsed in the garden. His temperature was 42°C (107.6°F). He was having seizures. We immediately started aggressive cooling: cool IV fluids, wet towels on belly, fans, and oxygen. After 30 minutes, his temperature dropped to 39.5°C, but he was still critical. Golu survived, but he needed three days in the ICU and had permanent kidney damage. His owner told us, “I thought Pugs were indoor dogs – I didn’t know a short walk could kill him.” Now Golu only goes out at 6 AM, for 5 minutes at a time. Don’t learn this lesson the hard way.

Which pets are at highest risk?

  • Brachycephalic breeds (Pugs, Bulldogs, Shih Tzus, Persians).
  • Senior pets and puppies.
  • Overweight pets – fat insulates and traps heat.
  • Pets with dark fur or thick double coats.
  • Pets with heart or respiratory disease.

Prevention – the best treatment

  • Walk only before 7 AM or after 7 PM (and still do the paw test).
  • Never leave your pet in a parked car – even with windows down, temperatures rise to lethal levels in minutes.
  • Provide constant access to fresh, cool water. Add ice cubes.
  • Create cool zones: tile floors, fans, cooling mats, or a damp towel for them to lie on.
  • Avoid muzzles during hot weather – they prevent panting.

Action for Week 18

  1. Memorise the early signs of heatstroke – panting, dark red gums, weakness. Act immediately if you see them.
  2. Practice cooling steps now (on a healthy day) so you’re ready in an emergency.
  3. Save our clinic’s location details in your phone for quick navigation.
  4. Share this information with family, walkers, and neighbours – heatstroke awareness saves lives.
