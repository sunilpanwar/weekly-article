🎨 Holi Safety – Protect Your Pet from Colours and Chemicals

Holi is a festival of joy, but colours and pets don't mix. Dry powders, wet colours, and even “natural” gulal can contain heavy metals, glass, or toxic dyes. When pets groom themselves, they ingest these harmful substances. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see an increase in Holi‑related emergencies every year – many of which are preventable.

🎨 Common sense rule: Keep pets indoors in a safe room during Holi play. If colour gets on them, wipe immediately with a damp cloth – never use chemical removers or scrubbing.

Why colours are dangerous

Many gulal powders contain lead, mercury, or silica. Wet colours may have engine oil or industrial dyes. Ingesting even a small amount causes vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage, and in severe cases, poisoning. The eyes are especially vulnerable – coloured powder can scratch the cornea or cause chemical burns.

Which pets are most at risk?

  • Outdoor pets – stray dogs, community cats, or pets with access to the garden.
  • Curious puppies and kittens – they explore with their mouths.
  • Pets with light‑coloured fur – colours show up more and owners may use harsher cleaning methods.

How to prepare your home for Holi

  • Designate a “safe room” – a quiet, closed room away from windows where colour play won't enter.
  • Close all windows and doors – coloured powder can drift inside.
  • Use calming aids – play soft music or use a pheromone diffuser (Adaptil for dogs, Feliway for cats).
  • Keep a pet emergency kit ready – saline eye wash, damp cloth, and our contact information.
🛡️ Dr. Deepa’s tip: “Apply a thin layer of coconut oil on your pet’s exposed skin (ears, paws, nose) before Holi – it repels colour and moisturises. Do this 30 minutes before any potential exposure.”

What to do if your pet gets coloured

  • Immediately wipe with a soft, damp cloth – no rubbing, just gentle dabbing.
  • Do not use soap or shampoo unless the colour is near eyes or mouth – that can spread it or cause irritation.
  • Rinse eyes with sterile saline solution (eye wash) for 5 minutes if colour enters.
  • If your pet vomits, seems lethargic, or has red eyes, bring them to the clinic immediately.

Safe cleanup after Holi

After the celebrations, give a gentle bath using a mild pet shampoo. Use lukewarm water – hot water sets stains. Never use kerosene, alcohol, or nail polish remover to clean your pet. If colour remains on fur, it will fade with time; do not scrub aggressively.

Real case from our clinic

Two years ago, a 6‑month‑old puppy, Chutki, was brought in after playing with wet Holi colours. She was vomiting, had red eyes, and was unusually lethargic. The owner had used a chemical colour remover on her fur. We treated Chutki for chemical poisoning and corneal abrasion. She recovered after three days of intensive care. The owner later said, “I didn’t know colours could be so toxic. Now I keep Chutki indoors during Holi.”

Helping stray animals on Holi

If you see a stray animal with colour on them, you can help:

  • Offer a bowl of clean water – they can rinse their eyes and mouth.
  • Do not try to bathe a frightened stray – you may get bitten.
  • Contact a local animal shelter or rescue group if the animal is in distress.

Action for Week 9

  1. Identify a safe room in your home. Prepare it with bedding, water, and toys.
  2. Buy pet‑safe eye wash (sterile saline). Keep it handy.
  3. Apply coconut oil to paws and ear tips before any outdoor exposure on Holi day.
  4. If you play Holi, change clothes before touching your pet – colours on your hands can transfer.
