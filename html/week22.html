⚠️ Hot Spots – Stop Constant Licking Before It Becomes a Wound

Does your pet lick one spot constantly – a leg, paw, tail, or flank? That seemingly innocent behaviour can turn into a painful, oozing wound called a “hot spot” (acute moist dermatitis) within hours. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we treat hot spots daily in summer and monsoon. The good news: they are preventable and treatable if you act quickly.

⚠️ Common sense rule: If your pet licks the same spot more than a few times in an hour, interrupt them. Use a cone (Elizabethan collar) if needed. A small itch can become a large infection overnight.

What is a hot spot?

A hot spot is an area of skin that becomes red, moist, swollen, and painful due to constant licking, chewing, or scratching. The saliva and moisture create a perfect environment for bacteria to grow. What starts as a tiny itch (from a flea bite, allergy, or even boredom) can become a raw, hairless patch the size of your palm within 24 hours.

Common causes of hot spots

  • Flea allergy dermatitis – one flea bite can trigger intense itching.
  • Seasonal allergies – pollen, grass, dust mites.
  • Food allergies – certain proteins or grains.
  • Ear infections – pets lick their paws or flank due to referred itch.
  • Boredom or stress – compulsive licking as a coping mechanism.
  • Underlying pain – arthritis or injury causing licking at the site.
  • Moisture trapped in fur – after swimming or rain, damp fur against skin breeds bacteria.

Signs of a hot spot

  • Red, raw, moist skin.
  • Hair loss in a circular or irregular patch.
  • Oozing, pus, or crusting.
  • Foul smell (bacterial infection).
  • Your pet yelps or flinches when you touch the area.
  • Constant licking, biting, or scratching at one spot.
🩺 Dr. Deepa’s warning: “Hot spots are extremely painful. If you touch one, your pet may snap or bite – not out of aggression, but because it hurts. Be gentle, and consider using a muzzle if you need to examine a painful area.”

First aid – what to do at home (mild cases)

  1. Stop the licking immediately. Use an Elizabethan collar (cone) or a soft inflatable collar. This is the most important step.
  2. Trim the fur around the area (if possible, with blunt scissors) to allow air circulation. Be careful not to cut the skin.
  3. Clean gently with diluted Betadine (1 part Betadine to 10 parts water) or chlorhexidine solution. Pat dry – do not rub.
  4. Apply a vet‑approved topical spray or cream (ask us which one). Never use human creams like hydrocortisone without guidance – they can worsen infection.
  5. Keep the area dry. Moisture is the enemy.

If the spot is larger than a 50 paise coin, very red, or oozing pus, do not treat at home – bring your pet to the clinic.

What NOT to do

  • Do not use human antiseptics (Dettol, Savlon, alcohol) – they burn and are toxic if licked.
  • Do not apply oil, ghee, or coconut oil – they trap moisture and make infection worse.
  • Do not let your pet lick “just a little” – saliva constantly reinfects the wound.
  • Do not wait to see if it heals on its own – hot spots expand rapidly.

Veterinary treatment for hot spots

At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we typically:

  • Shave and clean the area thoroughly under light sedation if painful.
  • Prescribe topical antibiotics and anti‑inflammatory creams.
  • Give oral antibiotics if the infection is deep.
  • Provide pain relief and anti‑itch medication.
  • Treat the underlying cause (fleas, allergies, ear infection).

Most hot spots heal within 7–10 days with proper treatment. Without treatment, they can spread and become systemic infections.

Real case from our clinic

A 4‑year‑old Labrador, Simba, came in with a hot spot on his hip the size of a cricket ball. The owner said, “Yesterday it was just a small red patch. Today it’s oozing and he won’t stop licking.” Simba had a flea allergy – just two fleas had triggered the reaction. We sedated him briefly, shaved and cleaned the area, and started antibiotics and anti‑itch medication. We also applied a flea preventive. Within 5 days, the hot spot had dried and started healing. The owner now uses year‑round flea control and checks Simba’s skin weekly.

Prevention – how to avoid hot spots

  • Use year‑round flea and tick prevention (ask us for recommendations).
  • Dry your pet thoroughly after baths, swimming, or rain – especially thick‑coated breeds.
  • Brush regularly to remove loose undercoat that traps moisture.
  • Manage allergies – we can help identify triggers and prescribe appropriate treatment.
  • Provide mental stimulation to prevent boredom‑licking (see Week 20).
  • Check your pet’s skin weekly – run your hands over their body to feel for bumps, scabs, or wet spots.

Breeds prone to hot spots

  • Labrador Retrievers
  • Golden Retrievers
  • German Shepherds
  • Indies (especially with allergies)
  • Any breed with a thick or dense coat

Action for Week 22

  1. Run your hands over your pet’s body right now. Feel for any damp, sticky, or crusty spots.
  2. If you find a red, moist patch, put a cone on your pet immediately and call us.
  3. Check your pet’s flea preventive status – is it up to date? If not, schedule a visit.
  4. Dry your pet thoroughly after any water exposure – use a towel or hairdryer on cool setting.
