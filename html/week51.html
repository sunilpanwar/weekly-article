💧 Winter Water – Pets Drink Less in Cold Weather, But They Still Need It

Winter brings cooler temperatures, and many pets naturally drink less water. They don’t feel as thirsty, and cold water may be less appealing. But dehydration in winter is just as dangerous as in summer – it leads to kidney strain, constipation, dry skin, and urinary tract issues. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we see pets every winter who develop health problems simply because they aren’t drinking enough. The solution is simple: make water more inviting, add moisture to food, and monitor intake.

💧 Common sense rule: Pets drink 30–50% less water in winter. Keep water at room temperature (not ice cold). Change it twice daily. Add warm water to kibble or offer wet food to increase fluid intake.

Why pets drink less in winter

  • Reduced thirst drive: Cold weather naturally suppresses thirst – pets simply don’t feel the need to drink.
  • Cold water aversion: Ice‑cold water can be unappealing or even cause stomach discomfort.
  • Less panting: Dogs lose less water through panting, so their body doesn’t signal thirst as strongly.
  • Indoor heating: Heaters dry out the air, but pets may not compensate by drinking more – they actually drink less.

Signs of dehydration in winter

  • Dry, sticky gums (instead of moist, pink gums).
  • Sunken eyes or dull corneas.
  • Loss of skin elasticity – gently pinch the skin at the back of the neck; it should snap back immediately. If it stays tented, your pet is dehydrated.
  • Lethargy, weakness, or reduced appetite.
  • Constipation or hard, dry stools.
  • Dark yellow or strong‑smelling urine – or less frequent urination.

If you notice these signs, encourage water intake immediately and bring your pet to us if they don’t improve.

🧊 Dr. Deepa’s tip: “In winter, serve water at room temperature or slightly warm (not hot). Let tap water sit for an hour to come to room temperature. Some pets prefer it that way.”

How to encourage your pet to drink more in winter

  • Warm the water slightly. Add a splash of warm water to the bowl – not hot, just tepid.
  • Change water twice daily. Stale water is even less appealing. Fresh, clean water encourages drinking.
  • Use a ceramic or stainless steel bowl. Plastic bowls can retain odours and are harder to clean.
  • Place multiple bowls around the house. In different rooms, especially near their favourite resting spots.
  • Consider a pet water fountain. Moving water is more interesting and often encourages drinking. (No brand promotion – just an option.)
  • Add flavour (safely). A teaspoon of low‑sodium chicken broth (no onion, no garlic) or a drop of tuna water (for cats) can make water irresistible.

Increase water through food – the easiest method

  • Add warm water to kibble. Pour ¼ cup of warm water over dry food and let it soak for a minute. Your pet will eat the food and get extra hydration.
  • Switch to wet food (or add wet to dry). Wet food is 75–80% water. Even mixing a spoonful of wet food with kibble adds significant moisture.
  • Offer broth or soup. Plain, unsalted, onion‑free chicken or bone broth served as a warm treat.
  • Ice cubes (if your pet likes them). Some pets enjoy licking or chewing ice cubes – a fun way to hydrate. Monitor to prevent choking.

Real case from our clinic

A 7‑year‑old Indie, Kalu, was brought in with constipation and lethargy in December. His owner said, “He’s hardly drinking. The water bowl stays full all day.” Kalu’s gums were tacky, and he was mildly dehydrated. We recommended adding warm water to his kibble and placing a second water bowl near his bed. Within two days, Kalu was drinking normally and his constipation resolved. His owner now adds water to every meal – “He doesn’t even notice, and it keeps him hydrated without forcing him to drink.”

What about cats?

Cats are even more prone to winter dehydration because they naturally have a low thirst drive (evolved to get water from prey). For cats:

  • Feed wet food as the primary diet – dry food in winter can worsen dehydration.
  • Use a ceramic or glass water bowl – cats dislike plastic.
  • Keep the water bowl away from their litter box and food – cats prefer separate locations.
  • Try a cat water fountain – many cats are attracted to running water.

When to worry – signs of kidney issues

If your pet drinks excessively (polydipsia) rather than less, that’s also a concern. Increased thirst can indicate:

  • Kidney disease
  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Hyperthyroidism (cats)
  • Urinary tract infection

If your pet is drinking more than usual – emptying the bowl multiple times a day – bring them for a checkup and blood work.

Action for Week 51

  1. Measure how much water your pet drinks in 24 hours (fill the bowl to a marked level, measure what’s left). A rough guide: 50–100 ml per kg per day.
  2. Switch to room‑temperature water today – let it sit out for an hour before serving.
  3. Add warm water to your pet’s kibble at the next meal – just enough to moisten.
  4. If your pet still isn’t drinking enough, schedule a wellness check – we’ll rule out medical causes and help you design a hydration plan.
