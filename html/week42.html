🧒 Kids & Pets – Never Touch a Dog While Sleeping or Eating

Most dog bites to children don’t come from aggressive strays – they come from the family dog, triggered by a simple, preventable mistake. At Dr. Deepa pet vet clinic, we treat children every year who were bitten because they startled a sleeping dog, reached into a food bowl, or hugged a dog who felt trapped. Teaching children basic pet safety rules can prevent pain, fear, and even euthanasia of a beloved pet.

🧒 Common sense rule: Teach children: “Never touch a dog while they are sleeping, eating, chewing a bone, or hiding.” Even the gentlest dog can react out of instinct when startled or guarding a resource.

Why dogs bite children – understanding triggers

  • Startle reflex: A sleeping dog may snap when woken suddenly – it’s an instinct, not aggression.
  • Resource guarding: Food, bones, or even a favourite toy can trigger a protective response.
  • Pain or illness: A dog with arthritis or an ear infection may bite when touched in a sore spot.
  • Fear or feeling trapped: Children often hug dogs tightly – many dogs find hugging threatening.
  • Overstimulation: Rough play, pulling tails or ears can push a dog past tolerance.

The 5 golden rules for kids (to memorise)

  1. Never touch a dog while sleeping. Wake a dog by calling their name from a safe distance.
  2. Never touch a dog while eating or chewing a bone. Give them space until they finish.
  3. Never take a toy or bone from a dog’s mouth. Ask an adult to trade it for a treat.
  4. Never hug or kiss a dog’s face. A gentle pet on the back or chest is safer.
  5. If a dog hides (under bed, in crate), leave them alone. They need quiet time.
🐕 Dr. Deepa’s tip: “Practice ‘red light / green light’ with your child: show them pictures of dogs sleeping, eating, playing, and hiding. Ask – can we touch now? Red light (no) vs green light (yes, after asking adult).”

How to teach children to approach a dog safely

  • Always ask the owner first: “May I pet your dog?” If the owner says no, respect that.
  • Let the dog see and sniff you first: Extend a closed fist (not fingers) – dogs can bite fingers that look like sausages.
  • Pet on the chest or side, not the head. Many dogs dislike hands coming over their head.
  • Keep face away from the dog’s face. No kissing, no nose‑to‑nose.
  • If the dog moves away, stop petting. They have said “no thanks”.

What to do if a dog approaches aggressively

Teach children the “Be a Tree” method (see Week 34):

  • Stand still, like a tree.
  • Fold branches (arms) across chest.
  • Look at roots (feet) – no eye contact.
  • Count slowly to ten – the dog usually walks away.
  • Never run, scream, or wave arms.

Real case from our clinic

A 6‑year‑old boy, Rohan, was bitten on the face by the family Labrador, Simba. Rohan had hugged Simba while he was sleeping. Simba woke up startled and snapped. The bite required stitches and left a scar. The parents considered giving Simba away. At our clinic, we explained that it was a startle reflex, not aggression – Simba had never bitten before. We worked with the family on safety rules. Rohan learned to call Simba’s name before approaching. Simba kept his bed in a quiet corner. Two years later, Rohan and Simba are best friends. The parents said, “We almost lost our dog because of a mistake we could have prevented.”

Teaching younger children (under 5)

Young children cannot reliably follow safety rules. Supervision is the only solution:

  • Never leave a young child alone with any dog – even the family pet.
  • Use baby gates to separate child and dog when you can’t supervise.
  • Do not allow the child to feed the dog or handle toys.

Signs a dog is uncomfortable – teach kids to recognise

  • Yawning when not tired (stress yawn).
  • Lip licking (not after food).
  • Tucked tail or ears back.
  • Whale eye (showing the white of the eye).
  • Growling – a warning, not “badness”.

If they see these signs, they should stop what they’re doing and move away slowly.

What about cats?

Cats also bite when startled or overstimulated. Teach children:

  • Never grab a cat’s tail or ears.
  • If a cat’s tail is twitching, stop petting – that’s a warning.
  • Let cats come to you – don’t chase or corner them.

Action for Week 42

  1. Today, have a family meeting – go over the 5 golden rules with all children.
  2. Practice “Be a Tree” stance together – make it a game.
  3. If your dog has ever growled or snapped at a child, schedule a behaviour consultation with us – do not wait for a bite.
  4. Supervise all interactions – even trusted pets can react under the wrong circumstances.
